🔥Calories

🔥What are calories?

Calories are a unit of energy also seen as (kcal) on food packaging. Since they are a form of energy when we consume food our bodies breaks the food down during digestion into a form of energy called ATP (adenosine triphosphate). That energy has to be stored somewhere in the body, as energy cannot be deleted, nor created as defined by the laws of physics.

Nutrition label showing calories per 100g in the top left hand corner, remember to always look at kcal (calories), not kJ.

🔥Where do calories go exactly?

Calories are always stored in the body. The 3 major tissues in the body where calories are stored are:

🧈Adipose tissue (fat)

🍞Glycogen (stored carbohydrates)

💪Muscle

🔥So why are calories so important?

Calories dictate what our weight does. Whether your weight goes up, down or stays the same is dictated by calories.

📈If you consume more calories than you burn = Weight gain (calorie surplus)

📉If you less caloreis than you burn = Weight loss (calorie deficit)

⚖️If consume the same number of calories that you are burning = Maintain weight (maintenance)

This means we can easily tell if we are at surplus, deficit or maintenance based on what direction our weight is heading. If we also track our calories, we know the exact number of calories we need to comnsume to gain, lose or maintain.

🔥How do we figure this out?

🤔Well, let's say you're consuming 2,000 calories per day as an easy example.

Your weight stays the same for several days, this means I am at maintenance.

🤓If I wanted to start losing 0.45kg per week from here I would simply reduce my calorie intake by 500 calories. Why? Because 0.45kg of fat = 3500kcal. If you want to lose 0.45kg per week you would be in a 3500kcal deficit across those 7 days. Simple maths: 500 x 7 = 3,500.

🧠Why does this happen? Well, when your body is in a calorie deficit it needs to get energy from somewhere to carry out its daily functions. It gets this energy from bodyfat, so you breakdown stored fat, 0.45kg of it = 3,500 calories worth. This means in a 500kcal daily for a week, you'd have broken down 0.45kg fat.

🔥Calories for building muscle

Calories are paramount for gaining muscle. Without a calorie surplus it is very hard to gain muscle at all, because an excess of energy is required to put towards building muscle. So during a fat loss phase you won't build muscle unless you are extremely new to training or taking anabolic steroids, which assist muscle growth and fat loss. This is dictated by a signalling process in the body called mTOR, which requires energy (calories) to be activated. If you are eating too few the body would prioritise keeping us alive, instead of building muscle.

🥩Protein

🥩What is protein?

Protein is a complex structure made from amino acids, peptide bonds, hydrogens and disfulfide bonds. To keep it simple, protein is made up of amino acids, amino acids link together with bonds called peptide bonds to form long strongs of amino acids called polypeptides. These polypeptides link together to form "protein". There are thousands of different proteins in the human body all with unique roles. The help our immune system function, builds tissues such as muscle, and connective tissues (ligaments, tendons, skin, cargtilage). Some even help fight disease.

🥩How are proteins made?

🧬Proteins are manufactured by cellular machinary called ribosomes, which are found in every single cell in our body. The nucleus is like the brain of the cell and it has a specific set of instructions for each individual cell. The cell nucleus contains DNA, and this DNA unzips its self forming mRNA. This mRNA travels to the ribosome and the ribosome reads the mRNA (single stranded DNA molecule) like a set of instructions and connects the required amino acid with transfer RNA (tRNA). This then produces a polypeptide, and later forms a protein via a process called post translation modification.

🥩Amino acids

🧠There are in total 20 amino acids, which can be divided into essential and non-essential amino acids.

👉Essential amino acids - You must consume them in your diet, the body does not produce them.

👉Non-essential amino acids - Your body can produce them, you do not have to consume these in your diet.

🥩Why is protein so important for muscle growth?

Muscles need protein in order to remodel themselves to become stronger and larger. During exercise mechanical tension is placed on a muscle, this mechanical tension causes an adaptation to take place causing sarcomeres to be added in parallel, called sarcomereogenesis, there is also the addition of proteins that cause muscles to contract (actin and myosin), which increase the size of muscle cells (myofibrillar hypertrophy).

If there is insufficient protein intake muscles are unable to properly adapt and recover from the previous sessions or sessions, which leads to either no growth, or delayed growth. Muscles use the amino acids mentioned above to remodel these muscle cells and add new elements within the muscle fibre to increase size.

🥩How much protein do we need?

We now know why protein is important for muscle growth and recovery, but how much do we actually need?

Based on current research we need, 1.6-2g/kg protein. This means per kilogram of our bodyweight we need to multiply that by 1.6-2g to get the total amount of protein needed. For most people this will be between 100 and 200g protein daily.

🥩Where should we get our protein from?

We can divide protein sources into two different categories - Animal based sources, and plant based sources. Both are great ways of getting in our protein, and it is a good idea to include both into your diet. Animal based sources tend to contain a better array of essential amino acids than plant based ones. However, there's currently a lack of evidence suggesting animal based protein is far superior to plant based. So just include both.

🥩Animal based sources:

🐟Fish: Salmon, tuna, cod, haddock, mackerel, sea bass, sardines, etc.

🍗Meat(preferably lean sources): Chicken breast, chicken thighs, legs, wings, lean beef mince (<10% fat), lean steaks, turkey breast, pork (sparringly), lamb (sparringly),

🥛Dairy: Milk, greek/skyr yoghurt, cheese, kefir, cottage cheese, quark.

🥚Eggs/egg whites

🥗 Plant based sources:

🫘Tofu, tempeh, beans and puluses (lentils, kidney beans, chickpeas etc), seitan

🥜Nuts such as almonds (high in leucine)

🥩Does it matter when you eat your protein?

Short answer is no, not really. However, we'll go into more detail. How often/when you eat your protein came from some research that found spreading protein out across the day increased muscle growth. This came from the knowledge that protein synthesis remains elevated for about 3-4 hours after a single meal of protein containing 0.25g/kg bodyweight (around 20 grams for most people). Bu, later research has found that eating more than this amount stimulates muscle protein synthesis even further, even amounts of up to 100g. This suggests that your total daily intake matters far more than how you spread your protein.

So my advice is don't go through the hassle of trying to time it perfectly throughout the day, and focus on achieving your total daily intake for the day instead.

🥩Facts about protein

🔍 Protein contains 4 calories per gram. Meaning if you eat 100 grams of protein you get 400kcal.

🔍 Protein has a thermic effect of 20%, meaning that when you eat protein about 20% of the calories go into the breakdown of it (360kcal instead of 400kcal).

🔍 Protein has a satiating effect, meaning it makes you feel full, which makes it great for losing weight, even if you're not trying to build muscle.

🔍 Protein is used all over the body, not just in muscle building. People see it as the muscle building macro, but it is way more than that.

🔍 Calories from protein still count, and you can gain fat by eating too much of it.

🥑Fats

🥑What are fats?

Fats are an essential macronutrient just like protein, which supports many functions within the body. They are made up of fatty acids, and be divided into saturated fats and unsaturated fats. We also have essential fatty acids, like we do essential amino acids.

The 4 key functions of fats

🥑 Hormone production. Many hormones, such as testosterone and estrogen require are made of fats.

🥑 Fats help us absorb fat soluble vitamins/minerals. Lack of fats in the diet will cause malabsorption of key vitamins/minerals.

🥑 Maintain the structure/function of cells and cell membranes.

🥑 Essential fatty acids help protect our heart, brain, blood vessels and maintain healthy eyesight and reduce our risk of diabetes

🥑Where do fats go?

After we've eaten a meal with fats in our small intestine absorbs the fatty acids, and delivers the fats to the liver. The liver sorts these out, some will go to tissues that need them immeediately and transported around in structures called chylomicrons. These can be distributed to adipose tissue to give us stored energy and a source of insulation.

🥑Types of fat

🧈Saturated fats

Saturated fats are fats that tend to be solid at room temperature, e.g. butter, lard, cheese, dark chocolate, and meats with fat in them like steak.

These almost always come from animals, and are known to increase our LDL cholesterol (bad cholesterol), although they're not inherently bad it is a good idea to try to limit saturated fat intake to no more than 10% of your energy intake.

🫒Unsaturated fats

Unsaturated fats are usually fluid, i.e. oil, a liquid at room temperature. E.g, olive oil, rapeseed oil, vegetable oil.

These oils almost always come from plants, although they're also found in oily fish such as salmon, sardines and mackerel. They are good for health, and help reduce LDL cholesterol, and increase HDL (good cholesterol).

🥑How much fat do we need?

We need a minimum of 0.5g/kg fat and ideally no more than 35% of our total daily energy intake as a maximum. This means if you are consuming 3,000 calories per day, you should have no more than 35% coming from fat. To calculate this simply do 3,000 x 0.35 = 1,050. 1,050/9 = 116.6g fat. Divided by 9 because there are 9 calories per gram of fat.

🥑Where should we get our fats from?

🥛Dairy - Kefir, cheese, cottagge cheese, greek yoghurt, milk

🥚Eggs

🐟 Fish - Salmon, mackerel, sardines

🫒Oils - Avocado oil, extra virgin olive oil, coconut oil

🥜Nuts and seeds - Sunflower seeds, chia seeds, flaxseed, pumpkin seeds, walnuts, almonds, cashew nuts

🥑Avocado

🥑Facts about fats

🔍 Fat contains 9 calories per gram, making it the most calorie dense macronutrient.

🔍 Fat is more likely to get stored as body fat when you are in a calorie surplus than protein and carbohydrate.

🔍 Fat is an essential nutrient you cannot live without essential fatty acids.

🔍 Fats are not bad for you, and they do not make you fat independently.

🍞Carbohydrates

🍞What are carbohydrates?

Carbohydrates are composed of sugar molecules. They can be very simple structures or very complex structures,this distinction is what determines how a carbohydrate behaves in the body. Carbohydrates are mainly seen as a fuel source that our body utilses very quickly. Unlike protein and fat, carbohydrates are a non-essential macronutrient, meaning we do not need them to survive.

The main functions of carbohydrates

🍞Provide the body with an immediate fuel source that is easy to breakdown.

🍞Can spare protein when there is a lack of protein, which promotes anabolic signalling.

🍞 Can contain fibre, which helps with bulking stool and promotes gut health.

🍞Various compounds and structures in the body use carbohydrates to make DNA, however, we don't need this from our diet, we can synthesise them.

🍞How are carbohydrates digested?

Mouth to blood

The digestion of carbohydrates actually begins in the mouth. Our mouth can secrete enzymes from salivary glands called "amylase", which breaks down carbs. The food travels down into the stomach where it is further browkn down by gastric juices, and into the small intestine. Digestion in the small intestine further breaksdown carbs with amylases. The sugar molecules can then pass through the lumen of the small intestine through enterocyte, and pass through into the blood via transporters.

Blood to cells

Now the sugar molecules are in the blood, it would be referred to as "blood glucos" or "blood sugar", this blood can be transported to tissues and therefore cells that need, this process is tightly regulated. Any left over glucose can be easily stored in the liver or muscles as glycogen.

The importance of blood sugar

Sugar or glucose in the blood is actually toxic if levels reach too high. So, our body likes to regulate glucose levels, so we don't die... It does this by using a hormone called "insulin". Insulin breaks down sugar molecules into energy, every cell uses insulin to get energy from glucose. If we had insufficient insulin, like seen in diabetes, we would get bio-accumulation of glucose, this is very dangerous and can cause limb damage, cardiovascular disease, kidney disease and nerve damage.

How important are carbohydrates for muscle growth?

Carbohydrates get a lot of stick for causing weight gain and fat gain. However, they are actually very important for muscle growth. This is because carbohydrates help us train hard, recover better and they are protein sparring. If we are able to train harder, recover better and spare protein we can simply produce more of a stimulus for muscle growth, recover from it and build more strength/muscle as a result.

Body composition benefits

Not only do carbohydrates enhance muscle growth, they also fill the muscles up with glycogen which makes them appear full. This can give the illusion of more muscle. When you cut down on carbs, you will notice your muscles deflate and this can actually mak your physique look worse at times.
