Calories and macronutrients

🔥 Calories

🔥 What are calories?

Calories are a unit of energy, also shown as (kcal) on food packaging. Since they are a form of energy, when we consume food our bodies break the food down during digestion into a form of energy called ATP (adenosine triphosphate). That energy has to be stored somewhere in the body, as energy cannot be created or destroyed according to the laws of physics.

Nutrition label showing calories per 100g in the top left-hand corner. Remember to always look at kcal (calories), not kJ.

🔥 Where do calories go exactly?

Calories are always stored in the body in some form. The 3 major tissues where calories are stored are:

🧈 Adipose tissue (fat)

🍞 Glycogen (stored carbohydrates)

💪 Muscle

🔥 So why are calories so important?

Calories dictate what our weight does. Whether your weight goes up, down, or stays the same is dictated by caloric intake versus expenditure.

📈 If you consume more calories than you burn = Weight gain (calorie surplus)

📉 If you consume fewer calories than you burn = Weight loss (calorie deficit)

⚖️ If you consume the same number of calories you burn = Maintain weight (maintenance)

This means we can easily tell if we are at surplus, deficit or maintenance based on what direction our weight is heading. If we also track our calories, we know the exact number of calories we need to consume to gain, lose or maintain.

🔥 How do we figure this out?

🤔 Let’s say you're consuming 2,000 calories per day as an easy example.

Your weight stays the same for several days — this means you are at maintenance.

🤓 If you wanted to start losing 0.45kg per week, you would simply reduce your calorie intake by 500 calories. Why? Because 0.45kg of fat = 3500 kcal. A 500 kcal daily deficit × 7 days = 3500 kcal.

🧠 When your body is in a calorie deficit it needs to get energy from somewhere to carry out its daily functions. It gets this energy from body fat, so you break down stored fat. A 500 kcal daily deficit for a week breaks down ~0.45kg of fat.

🔥 Calories for building muscle

Calories are paramount for gaining muscle. Without a calorie surplus it is very hard to gain muscle at all, because an excess of energy is required to put towards building muscle.

During a fat loss phase you won't build muscle unless you are extremely new to training or taking anabolic steroids, which assist muscle growth and fat loss.

This is dictated by a signalling process in the body called mTOR, which requires energy (calories) to be activated. If you are eating too few, the body prioritises survival over muscle building.

🔥 How do we burn calories? - Energy expenditure explained

We refer to energy expenditure as Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE). This dictates how many calories we burn day to day, and therefore influences how much we need to eat to gain or lose weight.

🔥 Key Components of Energy Expenditure

🛌 BMR (Basal Metabolic Rate) — Energy burned at rest (heart, immune system, breathing). ~60% of TDEE.

🚶 NEAT — Daily movement: chores, walking, fidgeting. ~20% of TDEE.

🍽️ TEF — Energy used to digest food. Protein and fibre increase this. ~10% of TDEE.

🏋️ EAT — Planned exercise: gym sessions, sports. ~10% of TDEE.

💡 Tip: Focus on increasing NEAT and fibre intake before relying on workouts to burn calories.

Common factors affecting energy expenditure

📏 Height

🧓 Age

⚖️ Weight

🏋️ Exercise/activity level

Less common factors affecting energy expenditure

⚠️ Thyroid conditions

⚠️ Cancers

🧬 Rare genetic metabolic differences

⚠️ Metabolic conditions such as diabetes

💊 Medications such as antipsychotics

🥩 Protein

🥩 What is protein?

Protein is a complex structure made from amino acids, peptide bonds, hydrogen atoms, and disulfide bonds. To keep it simple, protein is made up of amino acids. These amino acids link together with peptide bonds to form long chains called polypeptides. These polypeptides fold and link together to form a functional protein.

There are thousands of different proteins in the human body, each with unique roles. They support immune function, build tissues such as muscle and connective tissues (ligaments, tendons, skin, cartilage), and some even help fight disease.

🥩 How are proteins made?

🧬 Proteins are manufactured by cellular machinery called ribosomes, which are found in every cell in the body. The nucleus acts as the control centre and contains DNA. This DNA unwinds to form mRNA, which then travels to the ribosome.

The ribosome reads the mRNA like a set of instructions and connects the required amino acids using transfer RNA (tRNA). This produces a polypeptide, which later becomes a functional protein through post‑translational modification.

🥩 Amino acids

🧠 There are 20 amino acids in total, divided into essential and non‑essential amino acids.

👉 Essential amino acids — You must consume them in your diet; the body cannot produce them.

👉 Non‑essential amino acids — Your body can produce them; you do not need to consume them directly.

🥩 Why is protein so important for muscle growth?

Muscles need protein in order to remodel themselves and become stronger and larger. During exercise, mechanical tension is placed on a muscle, triggering adaptations such as adding sarcomeres in parallel (sarcomereogenesis) and increasing contractile proteins (actin and myosin), which leads to myofibrillar hypertrophy.

If protein intake is insufficient, muscles cannot properly adapt or recover, leading to little or no growth. Muscles rely on amino acids to remodel and add new structural elements within the muscle fibre.

🥩 How much protein do we need?

Based on current research, we need around 1.6–2 g/kg of bodyweight. Multiply your bodyweight in kilograms by 1.6–2 to get your daily protein target.

For most people, this falls between 100–200 g of protein per day.

🥩 Where should we get our protein from?

Protein sources can be divided into two categories: animal‑based and plant‑based. Both are effective ways to meet your protein needs, and including both in your diet is ideal.

Animal sources tend to contain a more complete array of essential amino acids, but current evidence does not show that animal protein is vastly superior to plant protein. Including both is perfectly fine.

🥩 Animal‑based sources:

🐟 Fish: salmon, tuna, cod, haddock, mackerel, sea bass, sardines, etc.

🍗 Meat (preferably lean): chicken breast/thighs, lean beef mince (<10% fat), steaks, turkey, pork (sparingly), lamb (sparingly)

🥛 Dairy: milk, Greek/Skyr yoghurt, cheese, kefir, cottage cheese, quark

🥚 Eggs/egg whites

🥗 Plant‑based sources:

🫘 Tofu, tempeh, beans, pulses (lentils, chickpeas, kidney beans), seitan

🥜 Nuts such as almonds (high in leucine)

🥩 Does it matter when you eat your protein?

Short answer: not really. Earlier research suggested that spreading protein across the day increased muscle growth because muscle protein synthesis stays elevated for 3–4 hours after a meal containing ~0.25 g/kg of protein.

But newer research shows that larger protein doses (even up to 100 g) stimulate muscle protein synthesis further. This means your total daily intake matters far more than timing.

So don’t stress about timing — focus on hitting your daily target.

🥩 Facts about protein

🔍 Protein contains 4 calories per gram.

🔍 Protein has a thermic effect of ~20% (360 kcal absorbed from 400 kcal eaten).

🔍 Protein is highly satiating — great for fat loss.

🔍 Protein is used all over the body, not just for muscle building.

🔍 Calories from protein still count — you can gain fat by overeating it.

🥑 Fats

🥑 What are fats?

Fats are an essential macronutrient, just like protein, and support many functions within the body. They are made up of fatty acids and can be divided into saturated fats and unsaturated fats. We also have essential fatty acids, similar to essential amino acids.

The 4 key functions of fats

🥑 Hormone production — many hormones such as testosterone and estrogen are made from fats.

🥑 Absorption of fat‑soluble vitamins and minerals — low fat intake can cause malabsorption.

🥑 Maintaining the structure and function of cells and cell membranes.

🥑 Essential fatty acids support the heart, brain, blood vessels, eyesight, and help reduce diabetes risk.

🥑 Where do fats go?

After eating a meal containing fats, the small intestine absorbs the fatty acids and delivers them to the liver. The liver sorts and distributes them — some go to tissues that need them immediately, transported in structures called chylomicrons.

Fatty acids can also be delivered to adipose tissue, where they provide stored energy and insulation.

🥑 Types of fat

🧈 Saturated fats

Saturated fats tend to be solid at room temperature — butter, lard, cheese, dark chocolate, and fatty meats like steak.

These mostly come from animals and are known to increase LDL cholesterol (bad cholesterol). They’re not inherently harmful, but it’s wise to limit saturated fat to no more than 10% of daily energy intake.

🫒 Unsaturated fats

Unsaturated fats are usually liquid at room temperature — olive oil, rapeseed oil, vegetable oil.

These mostly come from plants, but are also found in oily fish such as salmon, sardines, and mackerel. They help reduce LDL cholesterol and increase HDL (good cholesterol).

🥑 How much fat do we need?

We need a minimum of 0.5 g/kg of fat, and ideally no more than 35% of total daily energy intake.

Example: If you consume 3,000 calories per day, no more than 35% should come from fat.

3,000 × 0.35 = 1,050 calories from fat
1,050 ÷ 9 = 116.6 g fat
(Fat contains 9 calories per gram.)

🥑 Where should we get our fats from?

🥛 Dairy — kefir, cheese, cottage cheese, Greek yoghurt, milk

🥚 Eggs

🐟 Fish — salmon, mackerel, sardines

🫒 Oils — avocado oil, extra virgin olive oil, coconut oil

🥜 Nuts & seeds — sunflower seeds, chia seeds, flaxseed, pumpkin seeds, walnuts, almonds, cashews

🥑 Avocado

🥑 Facts about fats

🔍 Fat contains 9 calories per gram — the most calorie‑dense macronutrient.

🔍 Fat is more likely to be stored as body fat when in a calorie surplus.

🔍 Fat is essential — you cannot live without essential fatty acids.

🔍 Fats are not bad for you and do not cause fat gain independently.

🍞 Carbohydrates

🍞 What are carbohydrates?

Carbohydrates are composed of sugar molecules. They can be very simple structures or very complex structures — this distinction determines how a carbohydrate behaves in the body.

Carbohydrates are mainly seen as a fuel source that our body utilises very quickly. Unlike protein and fat, carbohydrates are a non‑essential macronutrient, meaning we do not need them to survive.

The main functions of carbohydrates

🍞 Provide the body with an immediate fuel source that is easy to break down.

🍞 Spare protein when dietary protein is low, promoting anabolic signalling.

🍞 Provide fibre, which helps bulk stool and promotes gut health.

🍞 Support the creation of various compounds and structures in the body (e.g., DNA), although we can synthesise these internally.

🍞 How are carbohydrates digested?

Mouth to blood

Carbohydrate digestion begins in the mouth. Salivary glands release amylase, which starts breaking down carbohydrates. Food then travels to the stomach for further breakdown, and into the small intestine where more amylases continue digestion.

Sugar molecules pass through the small intestine wall (enterocytes) and enter the bloodstream via transporters.

Once in the blood, sugar is referred to as “blood glucose”. It is transported to tissues and cells that need it. Any leftover glucose is stored in the liver or muscles as glycogen.

The importance of blood sugar

High blood sugar is toxic. The body regulates glucose levels using insulin, which allows cells to absorb glucose for energy. Without enough insulin — as seen in diabetes — glucose accumulates in the blood, causing serious complications such as nerve damage, kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, and limb damage.

🍞 How important are carbohydrates for muscle growth?

Carbohydrates often get blamed for weight gain, but they are extremely important for muscle growth. They help us train harder, recover better, and spare protein — all of which enhance muscle growth.

Body composition benefits

Carbohydrates fill muscles with glycogen, making them appear fuller. When carb intake drops, muscles deflate, which can make your physique look flatter even if you haven’t lost muscle.

🍞 How much do you need to eat?

Most people need 3–5 g/kg bodyweight. Carbohydrate intake should be tailored to activity levels:

🏃 Higher activity → closer to 5 g/kg

🛋️ Lower activity → closer to 3 g/kg

To calculate this, multiply your bodyweight in kilograms by 3–5 depending on your activity level.

🍞 What types of carbohydrate are there?

🚀 Simple sugars

Simple sugars digest rapidly and spike blood sugar levels. They are useful before or after exercise to replenish glycogen.

Monosaccharides — one sugar molecule (glucose, galactose, fructose)

Disaccharides — two sugar molecules (lactose, sucrose, maltose)

🐌 Complex carbohydrates

Slower digesting, slower rise in blood sugar, and should make up most of your diet.

Oligosaccharides — raffinose, stachyose, verbascose (3–10 sugar units)

Polysaccharides — starches like amylose, amylopectin, pectin, cellulose (10+ units)

Insoluble fibre, resistant starch

Soluble fibre

🍞 Where should we get our carbohydrates from?

Carbohydrate Intake Breakdown

85–90% of carb intake should be:

Primarily from:

Wholegrains: barley, oats, quinoa, wholegrain breads, wholegrain rice, wholegrain pasta.

Legumes: chickpeas, kidney beans, navy beans, edamame, lentils.

Fruit & veg: bananas, potatoes, mango, orange, apple.

Others: honey, milk, fresh orange juice and other fresh fruit juices.

10–15% can be:

Smaller portion from:

Cereals, white breads, donuts, cake, sweets, chocolate.

🍞 Facts about carbohydrates

🔍 Carbohydrates are made up of sugar molecules.

🔍 They contain 4 calories per gram.

🔍 Carbs are not bad for you, despite what social media often claims.

🔍 Carbs are not an essential nutrient — we can survive without them.

🔍 They are critical for performing well in the gym and recovering, just like protein.

🫘 Fibre

🫘 What is fibre?

Fibre is a type of carbohydrate that we cannot digest.

🫘 Soluble fibre

Soluble fibre passes from the mouth into the large intestine, where bacteria feed on it. It is quite literally food for the gut microbiome, helping beneficial bacteria grow. Soluble fibre produces a by‑product called short‑chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which are incredibly important for health as they reduce insulin resistance, provide anti‑cancer effects, and have anti‑inflammatory properties.

🫘 Insoluble fibre / resistant starch

Insoluble fibre and resistant starch resist digestion in the large intestine and pass out in your stool (think sweetcorn). Their main purpose is to bulk stool and help it pass more easily, and they may also help sweep carcinogens away from the colon.

🫘 How important is fibre for body composition & muscle growth?

Fibre & muscle growth

Fibre may support recovery and therefore muscle growth through its anti‑inflammatory properties and positive effects on insulin sensitivity.

Fibre for fat loss & body composition

Fibre forms a gel‑like substance in the digestive system, slowing digestion and increasing fullness — ideal for fat loss. Even when bulking, fibre remains important for overall health.

🫘 How much fibre do we need?

The recommendation is around 30 grams per day, but going above this is not harmful and may provide additional benefits as long as it does not cause digestive issues.

Fibre‑rich foods include: nuts, seeds, beans, lentils, fruits, and vegetables. Aim for a variety of fibre sources in your diet.

🫘 Facts about fibre

🔍 Fibre consists of indigestible carbohydrates.

🔍 Fibre is one of the most important components of our diet for optimal health.

🔍 Around 10–20% of the calories from high‑fibre foods are not absorbed.

🔍 When soluble fibre is broken down by bacteria in the colon, it produces hydrogen and methane — this is why fibre increases gas production.
