Recovery

What is recovery?

Recovery can be defined as the process of performance returning to baseline after a training session or group of training sessions. Training alwyas creates some level of fatigue, which causes a decrease in performance, for example reduction in strength. When our body is recovering it is still in a fatigued state and as a result performance will not be the same as usual. Once recovery has taken place, performance will return to "normal" and even get better, this is called adaptation. Adaptation is what makes us stronger, bigger, faster, etc... It's the exact thing we want, it's the process of the body getting fitter according to the stimulus that was given to it. The important thing to understand is that this adaptation will not happen if you are in a constant fatigued state, and under-recovered meaning you need to recover to get better. This is something a lot of people misunderstand. The diagram above illustrates how when we train there is a dip in performance after a training stimulus, which is caused by fatigue. But, during recovery we can "supercompensate" (get better essentially) due to the adaptation that has happened. If we don't train soon after we can get a reduction in performance, because there has been no stimulus recently, this is called "detraining" or "involution".

Each part of the body has its own recovery demands and time taken to recover. Usually when we think of recovering we think of making sore muscles less sore, this can be a decent marker of recovery. However, sometimes muscles can be sore, but recovery has taken place.

Protein

Protein amount

Protein is one of the most obvious factors to improve recovery. Protein helps muscles build new proteins and create new sacromere/myofibril units. As described in the previous section we need 1.6-2g/kg bodyweight protein per day to optimise this process. If we eat less than this we risk recovery being slower, and therefore our progress being slower, although we will still make progress. It's important to make the distinction that the more exercise you do, generally the more protein you need and the type of exercise matters too. Resistance exercise requires more protein than endurnace exercise in general, because it damages muscles much more easily and demands more protein to create those adaptations. Endurance athletes can get away with protein intakes between 1.2-1.6g/kg bodyweight.

Protein quality and type

To optimise this process it is generally recommended that we seek foods with complete amino acid profiles, because they give the body all the amino acids it needs in order to recover. This typically means animal proteins, which have the highest absorption rate according to current science, we use the DIAAS and PDCAAS score to define this.

Protein timing

Protein timing is a factor that may influence recovery. Although the evidence in the scientific literature is currently mixed on this, some suggesting that protein timing can be beneficial for enhancing muscle growth and recovery, and other research suggesting no difference. There has been a great deal of history from where the idea of protein timing stemmed from. Bodybuilders decades ago split their protein intakes across the day, because they believed it enhanced muscle growth for them, this lead research to kick off on the topic.

For protein timing we should aim to eat 0.25-0.5g/kg protein per meal, and those meals should be spaced about 3 hours apart. This means for most people around 15-50g protein per meal and each meal not being less than 3 hours apart. This is quite restrictive for most people's lifestyles and generally I wouldn't recommend it, but if you really enjoy doing this then it definitely does not hurt, and could yield you with improved recovery. The theory behind this is that protein synthesis spikes after a meal, but it comes back down quite sharply, if you have another protein meal it will spike it back up rather than flat lining for some time. Carbohydrates

Carbohydrate amount

Carbohydrates can improve recovery from exercise quite drastically, and it is important we eat enough of them to ensure we are getting the benefit they can give. Most people will benefit at 3-5g/kg bodyweight carbohydrate per day. Carbohydrates replenish glycogen stores in the body, which is an essential stored energy source for muscles to use during exercise and after to kick start the recovery process. After exercise there is an increase in a receptor found in muscle cells called the GLUT-4 receptor. This recpetor is crucial for muscle recovery, because it allows nutrients into the muscle after exercsie and increases insulin sensitivity to allow for more carbohydrate metabolism in this post workout window, which lasts around 60 mins.

Post workout carbs

Post workout carbohydrates should typically be moderate to high GI

  • Sports drinks like gatorade, powerade, lucozade
  • Fast digesting fruit like banana, dates, oranges
  • Sports powders like dextrose, maltodextrin, highly branched cyclic dextrin
  • Sugary cereals
  • Honey, jam
  • Cream of rice/ground rice
  • Skimmed milk

    • Carbohydrate timing

    As suggested above the timing of carbohydrate intake is fairly important for optimising recovery. Due to this GLUT-4 receptor activity after exercise, it is important that you are getting sufficient carbohydrates after exercise to enhance the recovery process. Ideally this is about 1g/kg bodyweight 60 minutes after exercise with an easy digesting carbohydrate source (i.e. simple carb containing). This can also be paired with a protein source, but is not required. This aspect becomes even more impoortant if you did exercise which depleted glycogen stores a lot such as endurance exercise lasting more than 45 minutes.

    Supplements

    Creatine monohydrate

    There's a large amount of evidence to support creatine boosting recovery from exercise. Take 5-10 grams daily with any drink (e.g. water) at any time of the day. It is unlikely that taking it after a workout has any extra benefit to taking it before, or much after. There's some evidence to suggest having it with a carbohydrate and protein source may help absorbption of creatine into your muscles, however again the chance of this making much difference is low. Creatine increases levels of phosphocreatine in our body, which helps convert ADP molecules into ATP molecules. ATP molecules are a key energy source for the body, and muscles require them during intense physical activity, exercise also depletes them. Creatine also increases intramuscular hydration, which likely part of the reason why it enhances recovery.

    Make sure to buy the monohydrate form it is the most researched!

    Whey protein

    Whey protein can help with recovery just like how protein in general helps with recovery. This benefit does not come from whey protein its self, but from the effect it has on total protein intake & protein timing. So there is no need to take a protein supplement if you are already consuming enough protein, and spreading your protein intake across the day.

    Curcumin + black pepper

    Curcumin is commonly known as a spice, and contains curcuminoids which are bioactive compounds which also bring down inflammation. Like anthocyanins, they are very powerful micronutrients, which can help recovery. It is responsible for that yellow colour. You can add curcumin to your food as a spice, or take a supplement of curcumin. Curcumin is not that bio-avaliable in it's original state, however, when mixed with black pepper it's absorption rate increases drastically. So in this case it should be added with black pepper, and the easiest way to do this would be to supplement it

    Micronutrients

    Omega-3

    Omega-3 can enhance recovery through its anti-inflammatory properties. Omega-3 is a type of healthy fat found in oily fish, walnuts, chia seeds and flax seed. It can also be supplemented, but I personally don't recommend supplementation of omega-3 because supplements tend to offer quite a low amount of omega-3 and are expensive. Therefore, it can be more efficient to get it through the diet with the additional benefits that come with it as you'll get other micronutrients when eating the foods that contain this omega-3. I would recommend over 1 gram per day of omega-3.

    Anthocyanins

    These are the purple/dark red pigment found in fruits and vegetables. They are incredibly powerful nutrients (technically a plant chemica). They are important because they have very strong antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory capacity. This helps muscles recover, because after exercise muscles inflame a lot, to bring down that inflammation anti-inflammatory properties in food can help. Tart cherries, blueberries, black berries, raspberries & beetroot are a prime example of foods that contain large amounts of this nutrient, so it is a good idea to have these in your diet frequently to help support recovery.

    Electrolytes - Ka, Na & Mg

    Having sufficient amounts of Potassium (Ka), Sodium (Na) and magnesium (Mg) is crucial for recovery as this ensures muscle cells are hydrated properly. Most people believe that water is the only thing responsible for hydration, and they are right that it is important, however the minerals sodium, potassium and magnesium are just as important and their balance is crucial. It is important that we get our diet right to get balanced levels of these. People commonly eat too much sodium, the Western diet is high in it. Potassium and magnesium are more commonly undereaten and that shouldn't be the case if you are eating properly. Eating a diet rich in fruits, vegetables, wholegrains and sea food will get you plenty of magnesium and potassium and you'll never even need to think about having a supplement. Remember that a more hydrated muscle is going to perform better, recover faster and is much less susceptible to injury.

    Vitamin D3

    There is fairly strong evidence that vitamin D3 helps with muscle recovery, as well as strengthening and supporting the recovery of bones and connective tissues. Vitamin D has strong effects on the immmune system, it reduces inflammation and has been shown to reduce markers of muscle damage after recovery like ALT (alanine aminotransferase) & AST (Aspartate aminotransferase). Athletes with higher levels of vitamin D had faster return to baseline performance and less delayed onset muscle soreness in general. Higher doses seem to benefit muscle recovery more than the general recommendation of 600IU. Doses of 4,000IU in the scientific literature have been shown to improve recovery, and it is generally recommended that individuals have 30–50 nmol/L serum vitamin D3 levels. I personally recommend supplementing with 2,000-4,000IU/day, especially during winter months. Food alone isn't sufficient to get enough vitamin D daily, and most people don't spend enough time outside to get high doses of vitamin D3. It is also worth knowing that vitamin D is important for the synthesis of testosterone, this might also give a slight performance & recovery edge.

    Hydration

    Hydration is the conrnerstone of recovery. Without sufficient water intake after or during exercise muscles are less effective at using energy from glycogen, and ATP which are key components of performance. But also, if hydration isn't restored properly muscles regain glycogen less efficiently, this slows down recovery because the muscle is not getting energy to properly recover fast enough. This means, just as it is important to eat enough carbohydrates after exercise to restore glycogen, it is important to hydrate too, otherwise some of that carbohydrate being consumed won't be utilised correctly.

    How much water do I need?

    This varies a lot depending on how hot your country it is, time of year, how often you train, how hard and long you train for. So instead of giving specific numbers it is best to learn to read your urine. Urine should be a pale yellow colour, a medium or dark yellow or even orange signify dehydration, clear urine suggests being overly hydrated.     What are the most hydrating fluids?

    Contary to belief, hydration is not as simple as drinking lots of water. Water is actually not as hydrating as most people think, this is because it's not very osmotically active. Water is one component that is important, however we need minerals such as sodium, potassium, chloride, and glucose to pass through into cells. Drinking water is all well and good, but if that water isn't passing into things that it should passing through you will not get the entire benefit. So even things like milk can be more hydrating than water, because they contain these key minerals along with carbohydrates to facilitate the movement across into cells.
