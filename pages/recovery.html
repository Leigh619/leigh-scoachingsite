Recovery

What is recovery?

Recovery can be defined as the process of performance returning to baseline after a training session or group of training sessions. Training alwyas creates some level of fatigue, which causes a decrease in performance, for example reduction in strength. When our body is recovering it is still in a fatigued state and as a result performance will not be the same as usual. Once recovery has taken place, performance will return to "normal" and even get better, this is called adaptation. Adaptation is what makes us stronger, bigger, faster, etc... It's the exact thing we want, it's the process of the body getting fitter according to the stimulus that was given to it. The important thing to understand is that this adaptation will not happen if you are in a constant fatigued state, and under-recovered meaning you need to recover to get better. This is something a lot of people misunderstand. The diagram above illustrates how when we train there is a dip in performance after a training stimulus, which is caused by fatigue. But, during recovery we can "supercompensate" (get better essentially) due to the adaptation that has happened. If we don't train soon after we can get a reduction in performance, because there has been no stimulus recently, this is called "detraining" or "involution".

Each part of the body has its own recovery demands and time taken to recover. Usually when we think of recovering we think of making sore muscles less sore, this can be a decent marker of recovery. However, sometimes muscles can be sore, but recovery has taken place.

Protein

Protein amount

Protein is one of the most obvious factors to improve recovery. Protein helps muscles build new proteins and create new sacromere/myofibril units. As described in the previous section we need 1.6-2g/kg bodyweight protein per day to optimise this process. If we eat less than this we risk recovery being slower, and therefore our progress being slower, although we will still make progress. It's important to make the distinction that the more exercise you do, generally the more protein you need and the type of exercise matters too. Resistance exercise requires more protein than endurnace exercise in general, because it damages muscles much more easily and demands more protein to create those adaptations. Endurance athletes can get away with protein intakes between 1.2-1.6g/kg bodyweight.

Protein quality and type

To optimise this process it is generally recommended that we seek foods with complete amino acid profiles, because they give the body all the amino acids it needs in order to recover. This typically means animal proteins, which have the highest absorption rate according to current science, we use the DIAAS and PDCAAS score to define this.

Protein timing

Protein timing is a factor that may influence recovery. Although the evidence in the scientific literature is currently mixed on this, some suggesting that protein timing can be beneficial for enhancing muscle growth and recovery, and other research suggesting no difference. There has been a great deal of history from where the idea of protein timing stemmed from. Bodybuilders decades ago split their protein intakes across the day, because they believed it enhanced muscle growth for them, this lead research to kick off on the topic.

For protein timing we should aim to eat 0.25-0.5g/kg protein per meal, and those meals should be spaced about 3 hours apart. This means for most people around 15-50g protein per meal and each meal not being less than 3 hours apart. This is quite restrictive for most people's lifestyles and generally I wouldn't recommend it, but if you really enjoy doing this then it definitely does not hurt, and could yield you with improved recovery. The theory behind this is that protein synthesis spikes after a meal, but it comes back down quite sharply, if you have another protein meal it will spike it back up rather than flat lining for some time.
