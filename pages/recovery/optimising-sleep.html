The importance of sleep

Sleep is not something that should be understated, or overlooked. For your fitness journey it is just as important what you eat, and the training program you have. Yet a lot don't treat it with the respect it deserves. Sleep can be the difference between making progress and not, and it directly effects recovery. Poor sleep blunts muscle protein synthesis rate (the process which controls muscle growth), it will supress mTORC1 signalling processes which control growth. It will make DOMS far more severe than usual (delayed on-set muscle soreness), performance will drop (i.e. you'll be weaker in the gym), you will feel tired, sluggish, and demotivated. These things all effect your progress quite significantly, however this is only a small piece to the problems lack of sleep can cause. There have been numerous studies showing that lack of sleep increases fat gain, and decrease muscle growth. This can be explained by the effects poor sleep has previously mentioned, however also due to the fact that sleep regulates appetite and testosterone production. A single night of poor sleep can increase ghrelin levels (hunger hormone) and cause overeating, or increase the cravings for sugary, salty and fatty foods which are more inclined to make you consume more calories. #

Circadian rhythms

Why are circadian rhythms important?

Circadian is latin for 24 hour cycle which regulates a living organisms physical, behavioural and mental changes, also known as "body clock". These circadian rhythms are important becuase they regulate how we feel and what we do on a daily basis. This is because our hormones are secreted based on this biological clock that exists, hormones such as testerone, cortisol, melatonin and many more are heavily influenced by the time of day. This is why we measure testosterone in the morning, because in the morning testosterone is always at its peak, we utilise these known principles to know exactly when we should take blood tests. Each hormone has a unique response to time of day, so not all hormones will be lowest in the morning. Of course this is important because hormones have a huge influence over what we do.

Typically, testosterone and cortisol peak in the morning. These levels gradually decrease throughout the day, and melatonin gradually rise. This is because cortisol stimulates the sympathetic nervous system we need cortisol to promote wakefullness. Melatonin levels rise throughout the day to promote restfullness, peaking at night when we want to sleep. The exact timings of these hormone secretions will depend on the individual and their body clock, as it depends on the factors which will be mentioned below. Blood pressure and body temperature are also influenced by circadian rhythms, where morning blood pressure is typically lowest, and highest in the evening, this is why it's important to measure blood pressure at different times of the day to ensure you're not getting inaccurate readings.

How do circadian rhythms work?

Circadian rhythms are dependent on a few key envrionmental stimuli. It is important to understand we have no conscious control over our internal body clock, it decides what time of day it is based on the stimuli. This is why jet lag exists.

These are:

🌞Light- sunlight, bluelight and red light.

🔊Sound - Noise can tell our internal body clock that it is day time

🍎Food - The time you eat meals can influence your circadian rhythms

🌡️Temperature - Generally speaking it is colder at night, this tells the body it is night time, and allows us to sleep, heat tends to suggest day time. Our body detects this and uses it to decide what time of day it is.

😰Stress - Stress typically happens during the day (evolutionarily) which promotes the stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system, which drives cortisol, adrenaline and noradrenaline levels up.

How this works to our advantage?

This is good because it means we can manipulate our own circadian rhythms to our advantage to get a particular effect. This is exactly why and how we get better control over our sleep.

Since sleep is dictated by circadian rhythms and we know we can influence when we secrete melatonin (the hormone that controls how sleepy we feel), we can predictably be tired every night at a specific time. To do this it is essential you have the same routine daily, otherwise you will find that how sleepy you feel and how energised you feel each day varies massively. We will discuss this in the next section about sleep habits.

Habits to that improve sleep

These are habits so to ensure they work, like any other habit, you must be doing them daily and consistently, otherwise you'll see no improvement in your sleep. I.e. this should just be part of your daily routine.

1. Exposing yourself to sunlight when you wake up

This helps set the circadian rhythm it tells the body that "it is day time". When you wake up go outside to get some vitamin D and fresh air, along with a morning coffee. If it is winter and there is minimal sunlight (e.g. overcast day) you can invest in a lamp with 10,000+ lux to provide you with sufficient light to stimulate the same sort of reaction.

This will cause cortisol levels and testosterone levels to increase and regulates melatonin release for the evening. This happens because light from the sun enters the retina (part of your eye) that energy and signal that is recieved from the retina passes through to a part of your brain called the Suprachiasmatic Nucleus, a part of your brain that regulates the internal body clock. This information from light kick-starts signalling pathways via HPA axis to the rest of the organs within your body through neural impulses being sent all over the body. E.g. this information would be used to allow the body to utilise its own circadian rhythm to regulate cortisol levels via the adrenal glands and, and likewise influencing the secretion of melatonin from the pineal gland.

2. Avoid caffeine past noon

Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors, this is how it makes us feel more awake and less sleepy. Adenosine is responsible for feeling rested and tired, if adenosine receptors are being blocked, adenosine molecules can no longer bind to them. This means that we know longer have its effects of sleepiness, thereby promoting wakefullness.

It is important that you avoid caffeine beyond a the afternoon because caffeine has quite a long half life of 4-6 hours. Half life is the amount of time it takes for half the dose of a compound or drug to dissapear from the blood. This means that if you took 200mg caffeine, 4-6 hours later there would be 100mg circulating in your blood, and in the next 4-6 hours after that there will be 50mg. This means after 8-12 hours there is still some caffeine in your blood, and quite a significant amount still if you took a large amount of caffeine. Caffeine has been shown to reduce/inhibit deep sleep, which is terrible because deep sleep is one of the most important parts of sleep, this is extremely high quality sleep that we need enough of, or we get a reduction in testosterone, increase in cortisol, anxiety, increased disease risk, reduced muscle protein synthesis, everything that we don't want and more...

Caffeine continued

Some people might say, "Well I can sleep with caffeine just fine, it doesn't effect me." This is a common statement used by people who often consume caffeine and they believe they are immune to the effects of caffeine. Deep sleep is still blocked in people who find they can fall asleep, this is because they are able to get into light sleep cycle, so it makes it seem like they're sleeping, but their sleep quality is very poor. Furthermore, there are numerous studies that show the effect of caffeine on sports performance at 3-6mg/kg bodyweight, some participants don't feel like they are any more energised but still have an effect from the caffeine (increased performance outcomes). This suggests that you don't always feel caffeines effect it can be going on in the background without you being completely conscious of it actually doing anything.

3. Consistent sleep schedule

You may misunderstand what is meant by a consistent sleep schedule, what this means is waking up & going to sleep at the same time daily. The key here is same time, and daily. There's no good varying the time you go to sleep and the time you wake up, and then wonder why you're feeling tired on a daily basis. It has been shown that even changes of 30 minutes in your bed time can significantly decrease sleep quality and raise your risk of cardiovascular disease, and ageing. Keeping the time you go to bed every night consistent is super important, assuming that you already get 7-9 hours sleep, but this goes without saying... The reason why this is so important is due to those circadian rhythms, when you change your sleep time or wake up time the body has to adjust each time you do that, and this causes a dysregulation. Your body likes predictability, changes set it off rhythm and disrupt hormone production as a result. If you do this, you'll notice a huge difference to how you feel, personally this was one of the biggest most impactful habits for me and my sleep.

4. Blue light exposure

Blue light is great for waking up, evolutionarily this is probably because we have blue skies in the morning so our retina & brain uses the bluelight from the sky in the morning to understand what time of day it is. The downside is that you don't need bluelight at night, and infact it will keep you awake. This is because viewing blue light can tell us that it is day time, and this information impacts what hormones we produce (we may start to secrete less melatonin, more adrenaline and cortisol).

At night this is not so good...It is important you avoid blue light, blue light often conmes from screens from technology. The good thing is we can turn on blue light filters on almost all our devices to reduce our blue light expsoure, this will help promote normal sleepiness at night as we start to secrete melatonin and less adrenaline/cortisol, giving us a much better night sleep.

Turning on filters

Turning on blue light filters on your phone or computer is pretty easy. Every device is slightly different the image will show how you do it on iphone.

Go to settings 👉 type "night shift" into the search bar 👉 Turn on scheduled and set a time that you want it to come on daily + whack the colour temperature up to most warm.

On windows computers: Press windows key 👉 Type "night light" 👉 Turn the strength up to max 👉 Set hours and choose the hours you want, I recommend 2-3 hours before you sleep.

5. Room conditions

Dark room, the lighting in your room plays a huge role on your ability to get off to sleep. The room should be as dark as possible to promote sleep, light can signal to the body that it is still day time, and will disrupt your ability to fall off to sleep.

Temperature, room temperature is really important. Your core body temperature needs to drop to get off to sleep, otherwise it's almost impossible to sleep. Hormones play a role in this, but just as important is your room temperature. If you live in a hot country it would be good idea to invest in an AC unit, or fan. Alternatively if you do not have these and you want to improve your ability to sleep, having a cold shower before going to sleep can help a lot, along with wetting parts of your body with cold water or a flannel can help.

Noise, as mentioned way above in the circadian rhythm section - our body uses noise to determine time of day, a quiet room is really important for getting off to sleep. So, if you live near a busy road shut windows, and eliminate as much noise as you can. You can also buy ear plugs and such to help get yourself off to sleep better.

6. Activating the parasympathetic nervous system

This has to be the most important one for people who suffer who have insomnia but are chronically stressed/anxious. It is highly likely that the reason why insomnia is happening is because your sympathetic nervous system is active before bed.

The parasympathetic nervous system is a part of the autonomic nervous system, which is usually referred to as "rest & digest" because the parasympathetic nervous system helps us secrete enzymes for digestion, and helps us rest/relax. This is due to the neurotransmitters that are produced during this state, it helps our resting HR decrease, improves GABA and melatonin secretion, lowers blood pressure, etc.

It is important to stimulate the parasympathetic nervous system 1-2 hours before bed. To do this you just simply have to engage in things which help you personally feel relaxed. This might be reading a book, meditating, watching a movie, taking a bath/shower. Anything that helps you relax is what you should be doing 1-2 hours before bed. If your sympathetic nervous system takes over and you are stressing/worrying about things before bed your body will begin to secrete adrenaline, and cortisol, your HR will go up, your blood pressure will increase and ultimately you won't fall asleep.

7. Magnesium supplementation

Magnesium has increased in popularity in the past few years as a sleep supplement, and there is good reason for that. Magnesium has a reasonable level of evidence behind it improving sleep quality. This is largely because magnesiunm can increase GABA production (the neurotransmitter for restfullness), which then goes onto increase melatonin production, enabling us to get off to sleep more quickly and to have a better quality night sleep.

In terms of supplementation 300-400mg, 30-60 minutes before bed is good. The form of magnesium you take is also important, magnesium citrateo or biglycinate are known as highly bioavaliable and researched forms, so I suggest those.
